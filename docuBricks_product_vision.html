DocuBricks — Expanded Product Vision

DocuBricks — Product Vision · 2026

The document is the interface.
The intelligence should be invisible.

A platform that turns the institutional knowledge locked inside PDFs, contracts, and forms into answers — without exposing a single line of SQL, a single Databricks cluster, or a single schema definition to the person asking the question.

● First Principles

What is a document, really?

Let us be precise. A document is a compressed, lossy encoding of a human decision or observation, serialised into a format optimised for legal durability — not for machine consumption. A mortgage application is a 47-field decision tree rendered as PDF. An ISDA master agreement is a graph of counterparty obligations serialised as prose. A clinical note is a physician's diagnostic reasoning compressed into SOAP format and then frozen.

"The extraction problem is fundamentally a decompression problem. The information is in there — it has always been in there. The question is whether you have the right supervision signal to recover it."

For decades the answer was human labour: armies of analysts, paralegals, and data-entry operators whose sole function was to decompress documents back into structured form. Then came OCR. Then template-based extraction. Then rule engines. Each generation got part of the way there. None got all the way, because none could generalise across the infinite variation of real documents.

Foundation models break this ceiling. They can generalise. But generalisation without a schema is noise. The schema is the supervision signal — it is what tells the model what to attend to, what to extract, and how to validate the output. The schema is the real engineering artefact. The model is, increasingly, a commodity.

The IP is not the pipeline. Autoloader, ai_parse_document, Delta Lake — these are infrastructure. Any team with a Databricks contract can wire them together. The IP is the 30+ domain-specific schemas that encode ACORD field semantics, CMS-1500 claim structures, ISDA schedule hierarchies, and HL7 FHIR observation codes. That knowledge took domain experts decades to accumulate. It is not replicable in a weekend.
The architecture is correct. You need: (1) a streaming ingestion layer that handles any file origin, (2) a parse step that turns bytes into tokens, (3) a schema-bound extraction step that turns tokens into structured rows, (4) a governed storage layer that makes those rows queryable and auditable, and (5) a serving layer that makes the rows answerable in plain English. These are the right separation of concerns. Each layer is independently replaceable. That is good architecture.
Lakebase closes a genuine gap. Delta Lake is a phenomenal analytical engine. It is not an operational database. Document processing is an operational workload: job state transitions, reprocessing queues, per-document audit trails, sub-second metadata reads. Postgres-on-Lakebase is the correct solution to this impedance mismatch. The fact that nobody has built this on Databricks yet is a first-mover window, not a warning sign.
◆ Design Philosophy

Complexity is not a feature. Invisibility is.

There is a temptation, when you have built something technically sophisticated, to let that sophistication show. To surface the cluster configuration, the schema editor, the pipeline DAG, the Unity Catalog lineage graph. To let the user feel the machinery.

"Resist this. Every piece of complexity you expose to the user is a failure of design. The question is not how much the system can do — the question is how little the user needs to know in order for it to do it."

The loan officer does not want to know which LLM extracted the debt-to-income ratio from page 7 of the mortgage application. She wants the answer. The compliance analyst does not want to configure a Unity Catalog schema for AML suspicious activity reports. He wants the flag to surface automatically. The CFO does not want a Genie workspace tour. She wants to ask "how many invoices over $50k were approved without dual sign-off last quarter" and receive an accurate number.

The design principle for DocuBricks is total infrastructure amnesia. Databricks is the engine room. The user experience is the deck. The two should never meet.

01
Zero onboarding friction
A new user connects a document source — SharePoint, S3, an email attachment — and the system classifies, extracts, and surfaces it. No schema configuration required. The schema is already there, pre-built for their vertical. The first document lands in under 10 minutes.
02
Questions, not queries
The interaction model is conversational. "Show me all KYC forms with missing PEP declarations." "Which contracts expire in the next 90 days?" These are not SQL queries; they are business questions. Genie translates them. The user never sees the translation.
03
Confidence, not noise
Every extracted field carries a confidence score. The system surfaces low-confidence extractions for human review — not as errors, but as refinements. The UI treats uncertainty as a natural part of the process, not a failure state. Users learn to trust the system's calibration.
04
The schema is a lens, not a cage
Pre-built schemas are starting points. Every enterprise has idiosyncratic documents — internal approval forms, proprietary contract structures, bespoke reporting templates. The platform must make schema extension feel like adjusting a preference, not filing an IT ticket.
05
Governance by default, not by request
Because everything runs inside Unity Catalog, governance is not a feature the user enables — it is a property of the architecture. Lineage, access controls, audit trails, and data residency compliance are automatically inherited. The CISO's requirements are satisfied before they are asked.
06
Every vertical deserves its own idiom
A healthcare administrator and a mortgage underwriter do not think about documents the same way. The platform's language — its labels, its default views, its alert vocabulary — should shift to match the vertical. The FS dashboard speaks FS; the clinical dashboard speaks clinical.

▲ The Core Insight

The schema library is the product. Everything else is plumbing.

The model changes. The Databricks APIs evolve. The pricing tiers get revised. The schema library compounds.

"Each new schema you ship is a month of regulatory research, domain expert interviews, and validation runs crystallised into a reusable artefact. It cannot be replicated overnight. It does not deprecate. It appreciates in value as it is refined by production traffic."

A schema for a CMS-1500 healthcare claim is not just a list of field names. It encodes which fields are required for which payer types, what values are valid for procedure codes, how to handle split-billing, and where to look when the document layout deviates from the standard. That knowledge lives in the schema prompt template, the validation rules, and the edge-case handling logic.

The business model follows directly from this insight. You are not selling a pipeline. You are selling an increasingly rich library of institutional intelligence — and the infrastructure to make that intelligence queryable in plain English.

● Systems Thinking

The flywheel that compounds the moat

The architectural advantage is durable because of a feedback loop that is often described but rarely implemented correctly. Here it is stated precisely:

More customers deploy schemas
→
More edge cases surface in production
→
MLflow eval harness captures failures
→
Schemas improve (prompts + rules)
→
Higher extraction accuracy → retention

The loop closes: higher accuracy produces higher retention, which means more data, which means more edge cases, which means better schemas. The first mover does not just get revenue — they get the training signal. A competitor entering 18 months later has to buy that learning with time, not money.

Model-agnostic extraction is not a feature — it is a systems bet. The model layer is commoditising rapidly. Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, Mistral, Meta — the capability gap between frontier models is narrowing every six months. The correct long-term position is to own the schema layer and be indifferent to which model executes against it. Customers can optimise cost vs. accuracy per document type without changing a single schema definition.
The evaluation harness is as important as the extraction pipeline. Without MLflow tracking per-field accuracy across document types, you cannot improve systematically. With it, you can identify exactly which field on exactly which document type is underperforming and iterate on the schema prompt until it passes the quality bar. This is the engineering discipline that separates a demo from a production system.
◆ Experience Design

Three moments that define the product

Products are remembered by their best moments. Design those moments first. Let the rest of the engineering serve them.

Moment 01 — First ten minutes
The document just appeared — correctly structured
A compliance officer uploads a KYC form. Without configuring anything, the system identifies it as a KYC document, extracts 23 fields, flags 2 as low-confidence, and surfaces them for review. The first experience of the product is competence — not configuration.
Moment 02 — The first question
"Show me all mortgage applications above $500k where the DTI ratio exceeds 43%"
The loan portfolio manager types this into Genie. Three seconds later: a table, a chart, a count. No SQL. No dashboard configuration. No IT ticket. This is the moment the user understands what they are working with — and it must be perfect.
Moment 03 — The agent acts
The contract expired and the system told someone
An Agent Bricks workflow monitors extracted contract expiry dates. 90 days before expiry, it surfaces the contract, the counterparty, the renewal terms, and the legal contact — without a human having to remember to check. The system becomes proactive. This is the moment it earns permanence.

Every other feature in the product roadmap should be evaluated by one question: does it make one of these three moments better, faster, or more reliable? If not, it belongs in a later release.

● MVP Discipline

The MVP: what is in, what is not, and why

An MVP that ships is worth infinitely more than a complete product that does not. The financial services vertical is the correct first beachhead: high regulatory document complexity (which justifies the price), structured document formats (tractable extraction problem), existing Databricks footprint in 60%+ of Fortune 500 FS firms (warm distribution), and high willingness to pay for accuracy (because the cost of a missed AML flag is not a support ticket — it is a regulatory fine).

Capability MVP v1 (Month 0–3) Rationale
Ingestion IN — S3 + SharePoint via Autoloader / Lakeflow Covers 80% of enterprise document sources in FS. Email connectors in v2.
Document parsing IN — ai_parse_document (PDF, DOCX, images) Native Databricks. No third-party OCR dependency. 3–5× cheaper than alternatives.
Schema bundles IN — FS vertical: mortgage app, KYC/CDD form, invoice, AML SAR Four document types covers ~70% of FS document volume. Focus beats breadth.
Extraction engine IN — ai_extract with schema-bound prompt templates Schema-bound extraction with confidence scoring. MLflow eval harness required at launch.
Storage IN — Delta Lake (extracted tables) + Lakebase (document registry) Both required for the architecture to be correct. Lakebase in preview — abstract behind interface.
Query interface IN — Genie workspace, pre-configured for FS schemas This is Moment 02. It must ship. Pre-built queries seed the experience.
Dashboards OUT — AI/BI pre-built dashboards Genie covers the query use case at MVP. Dashboards are a Professional-tier upsell.
Agent workflows OUT — Agent Bricks multi-step automation Moment 03 is compelling but not required for FS MVP. Ship in Month 6 with enterprise tier.
Multi-vertical OUT — Healthcare, legal, manufacturing schemas Depth in FS beats breadth across verticals. Each vertical is its own go-to-market motion.
Schema editor UI OUT — Self-service schema customisation Custom schemas delivered as professional services in v1. Self-service UI in v2 after pattern emerges.
Human-in-loop review IN — Low-confidence field review queue Required for regulated documents. Simple queue UI. Not a full annotation platform.

"Scope is a design decision, not a resource constraint. Choosing what is out is as important as choosing what is in — and it requires more conviction."

▲ The Vision

What this becomes in five years

In five years, DocuBricks is the institutional memory layer for regulated enterprises. Every document an organisation has ever processed — every loan application, every clinical note, every contract, every regulatory filing — is a row in a governed table, queryable in plain English, traceable to its source, and auditable by any regulator who asks.

The document processing industry spends approximately $14 billion per year on human labour to do what DocuBricks will do automatically. That labour is not replaced overnight — but it is redirected. The loan analyst who spent 60% of her time pulling data from documents now spends 60% of her time acting on the insights those documents contain. That is not a marginal improvement. That is a fundamental reallocation of human attention toward human judgment.

"The goal is not to build a better document processor. The goal is to make every organisation as intelligent about its own documents as the people who wrote them."

The path there starts with four document types, one vertical, and three design-partner banks. It starts with a compliance officer uploading a KYC form and seeing it appear — correctly, completely, confidently — without configuring a single thing. It starts with that moment. Build that moment perfectly, and everything else follows.
